1. Briefly write about Human Psychology? Explain the nature and scope of Psychology?

Human psychology is the scientific study of human behaviour and mental processes. The word comes from the Greek psyche (mind or soul) and logos (study). Behaviour covers everything a person does that can be observed (speaking, walking, crying), and mental processes cover what goes on inside (thinking, remembering, feeling, perceiving). Human psychology tries to understand why people think, feel and act as they do.

Goals of psychology

  • Describe behaviour accurately.
  • Explain why it happens.
  • Predict how a person is likely to behave in a given situation.
  • Control or modify behaviour to improve people's lives (for example, reducing a fear or building a study habit).

Nature of psychology

  • Scientific: it uses systematic methods such as observation, experiments, surveys and case studies, and its conclusions are based on evidence, not opinion.
  • Study of both behaviour and mind: early psychology studied the mind through introspection; behaviourism studied only observable behaviour; modern psychology studies both.
  • Positive science and applied science: it develops theories and also applies them to practical problems in schools, hospitals and industry.
  • Interdisciplinary: it draws on biology, sociology, anthropology, medicine and computer science.
  • Individual differences: no two people respond in exactly the same way, and psychology studies what is common as well as what is different.

Scope of psychology

The scope is the range of areas in which psychology is studied and used, and it is divided into branches.

3. Distinguish between the Human Behaviour and individual behaviour?

Human behaviour means the general ways in which human beings in all societies think, feel and act, the patterns that are shared by most people. Individual behaviour means the particular way in which one person behaves, which makes him or her different from everyone else. Psychology studies both: it first finds the general laws, and then explains how and why individuals differ.

Factors that make individuals different

  • Heredity: inherited traits such as temperament and appearance.
  • Environment: family, school, friends, culture, economic conditions.
  • Personality and motives: values, interests, needs, self-concept.
  • Abilities and experiences: intelligence, skills and past learning.

Therefore every individual behaviour is an expression of general human tendencies shaped by personal factors; a teacher uses general principles of learning but must still treat each student as an individual.

4. Compare between the major learning theories: Classical Conditioning, Operant Conditioning?

Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour that results from experience. Classical and operant conditioning are two basic types of associative learning, one connecting stimuli with each other and the other connecting behaviour with its consequences.

Classical conditioning (Ivan Pavlov)

A neutral stimulus is repeatedly paired with a stimulus that naturally produces a response, until the neutral stimulus alone produces that response. In Pavlov's experiment, food (unconditioned stimulus, UCS) naturally made a dog salivate (unconditioned response, UCR). A bell (neutral stimulus) was sounded just before the food. After many pairings the bell alone (now conditioned stimulus, CS) caused salivation (conditioned response, CR).

Operant conditioning (B. F. Skinner, E. L. Thorndike)

Behaviour is shaped by its consequences: behaviour followed by a reward becomes more likely, and behaviour followed by an unpleasant result becomes less likely. A rat in Skinner's box learns that pressing a lever gives food, so it presses more often. The sequence is behaviour → consequence → change in future behaviour (Thorndike's Law of Effect).

Add somethingRemove something
Increase behaviourPositive reinforcement: praise for a good gradeNegative reinforcement: the alarm stops when you get up
Decrease behaviourPositive punishment: extra homework for misbehaviourNegative punishment: phone taken away for rule-breaking

5. Explain about Observational Learning with a suitable example.

Observational learning (also called social learning or modelling) is learning by watching the behaviour of other people, called models, and the consequences of their behaviour, without direct reward or punishment to oneself. It was explained by Albert Bandura in his Social Learning Theory, who argued that learning is not only the result of conditioning but also of watching others.

Bandura's Bobo doll experiment (1961)

Children watched an adult hit and shout at an inflatable Bobo doll, while another group watched a non-aggressive adult. When later left alone with the doll, the children who had seen the aggressive model copied the same actions and words, even though they had not been rewarded for doing so. This showed that behaviour can be learned simply by observation.

Four processes involved

  1. Attention: the learner must notice the model. Models who are attractive, similar, respected or powerful get more attention.
  2. Retention: the observed behaviour must be remembered, as images or words.
  3. Reproduction: the learner must be physically and mentally able to carry out the behaviour.
  4. Motivation: the learner copies the behaviour if there is a reason, mainly vicarious reinforcement (seeing the model rewarded) or expected rewards. If the model is punished, imitation is less likely (vicarious punishment).

Types of models

Live models (parents, teachers, friends), symbolic models (television, films, social media, characters in books) and verbal models (instructions or descriptions).

Examples

  • A child learns to speak politeness words and table manners by watching parents.
  • A trainee engineer learns to operate a machine by watching a senior technician before trying it.
  • Students become punctual after seeing a punctual and respected teacher get appreciated.
  • Teenagers copy the style and language of social media influencers (a symbolic model).

Importance

It explains how attitudes, values, skills and even fears are acquired in families, schools and workplaces. It supports the use of role models, demonstrations, mentoring and training videos, and also warns that violent or harmful content can be imitated.

6. Distinguish between Good Habits and Self-Discipline? Explain their importance in academic and professional life?

A habit is a behaviour that has been repeated so often in the same situation that it becomes automatic and needs little thought or effort (brushing teeth, checking the phone). Self-discipline is the ability to control one's own impulses, emotions and actions in order to do what is needed to reach a goal, even when one does not feel like it.

How a habit forms

A habit is built by a loop: a cue triggers a routine, which brings a reward. Repeating the loop strengthens it. To build a good habit, attach it to a clear cue, start small, repeat daily and reward progress.

Importance in academic life

  • Regular study, timely assignments and attendance improve grades and reduce last-minute stress.
  • Discipline helps resist distractions such as mobile phones and social media.
  • Good habits like reading, note-making and self-testing build long-term learning.

Importance in professional life

  • Punctuality, meeting deadlines and consistency build trust with employers and clients.
  • Continuous skill improvement and time management lead to higher productivity and career growth.
  • Self-control in anger and stress improves teamwork and leadership.

In short: talent and motivation vary day to day, but habits supported by discipline keep a person moving towards the goal.

7. Define Motivation and Explain the importance of Motivation and Goal Setting?

Motivation is the internal state or process that arouses, directs and sustains goal-directed behaviour. It answers why a person starts an action, how much effort is put in and how long it is continued. It comes from needs, drives, interests, rewards and goals. Example: a student studies late at night because she wants to become an engineer.

Importance of motivation

  • Starts behaviour: it gives the energy to begin a task.
  • Directs effort towards chosen goals and away from distractions.
  • Sustains persistence: motivated people continue despite difficulty and failure.
  • Improves performance and learning in studies, sport and work.
  • Supports well-being: purpose and satisfaction reduce boredom and stress.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Maslow explained motivation as a ladder of needs: lower needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher ones become strong motivators.

Goal setting

A goal is the desired result that a person plans and commits to achieve. According to Locke and Latham's goal-setting theory, specific and challenging goals, with commitment and feedback, produce higher performance than vague goals like “do your best”. Goals are best set using the SMART method:

Importance of goal setting

  • Gives direction, focus and a sense of purpose.
  • Increases motivation because progress is visible; short-term goals build confidence for long-term ones.
  • Helps in planning time and decisions, and allows self-evaluation and feedback.

The two kinds of motivation differ in where the reason for action comes from: from within the person or from outside.

Important points

  • Overjustification effect: giving external rewards for something a person already enjoys can reduce the inner interest (a child who loved drawing draws less once she is paid for it).
  • Self-Determination Theory (Deci and Ryan) says intrinsic motivation grows when three needs are met: autonomy (choice), competence (feeling capable) and relatedness (belonging).
  • Both are useful: extrinsic rewards help start boring but necessary tasks (paying taxes, routine lab reports), and good teachers and managers use them to begin with while building interest, choice and meaning for the long term.

10. Explain the major learning theories: Classical Conditioning, Operant Conditioning, and Observational Learning, with suitable examples.

Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour or knowledge that results from experience. Psychologists describe three major ways in which learning takes place.

1. Classical conditioning (Ivan Pavlov)

Learning by association of stimuli. A neutral stimulus (NS) is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus (UCS) that naturally produces an unconditioned response (UCR). Finally the neutral stimulus alone produces the response and is called the conditioned stimulus (CS), and the response is the conditioned response (CR).

  • Example: Pavlov's dog salivated at a bell that had always come before food. A student who was scolded on stage in the past feels anxious (CR) at every presentation (CS).
  • Key terms: acquisition, extinction (the response fades when the CS is no longer paired with the UCS), spontaneous recovery, generalisation and discrimination.

2. Operant conditioning (B. F. Skinner)

Learning by consequences: voluntary behaviour that is followed by a reward (reinforcement) is repeated, while behaviour followed by punishment is reduced.

  • Positive reinforcement: adding a pleasant thing, like praise or a bonus, after good work.
  • Negative reinforcement: removing an unpleasant thing, such as a seat belt alarm that stops when the belt is fastened.
  • Punishment: adding something unpleasant or removing something pleasant to reduce behaviour.
  • Example: a student who earns a gift for each good test result studies harder.
  • Schedules of reinforcement: continuous (every time) or partial (sometimes), where partial rewards, as in games or slot machines, make behaviour strongest and hardest to extinguish.

3. Observational learning (Albert Bandura)

Learning by watching and imitating a model without direct reinforcement. It needs attention, retention, reproduction and motivation. In the Bobo doll experiment, children copied the aggression they had seen in an adult.

  • Example: a junior programmer learns code review habits by watching a senior; children copy the language of film characters.
