11. Write about problem solving. Explain the problem-solving process with suitable examples.

Problem solving is the mental process of finding a way to reach a goal when the path to it is not immediately clear. A problem exists when there is a gap between the present state and the desired state, and the person has to think to close the gap. Examples: a program that gives wrong output, a clash in the exam timetable, or a bridge design that exceeds the budget.

Problem-solving process

  1. Identify the problem: notice that something is wrong or a goal is not met.
  2. Define it clearly: state exactly what the problem is, its limits and its causes (the difference between a “slow app” and “the login page takes 8 seconds”).
  3. Explore and generate possible solutions: collect information, brainstorm many options without judging them.
  4. Choose and act: evaluate the options by cost, time and chances of success, pick the best one and implement it.
  5. Look back and evaluate: check whether the goal was reached; if not, return to an earlier step.

Example: a student's mobile does not switch on. Identify: phone dead. Define: it did not start after the charge. Explore: battery empty, faulty charger, hardware fault. Act: test with another charger, then reset the battery. Look back: it works; keep a spare charger.

Strategies

  • Trial and error: trying options until one works (simple problems).
  • Algorithm: a step-by-step procedure that guarantees a solution (a formula, a debugging checklist).
  • Heuristics: rules of thumb that save time but may fail (working backwards, breaking the problem into sub-goals, means-end analysis).
  • Insight: a sudden realisation of the solution (the “aha” moment).

Barriers

Mental set (sticking to a method that worked before), functional fixedness (seeing an object only in its usual use, like not using a coin as a screwdriver), lack of information, fear of failure and emotional stress. Good problem solvers stay flexible, question assumptions and look at the problem from different angles.

13. Define sensation and perception. Explain the difference between sensation and perception

Sensation is the process by which the sense organs (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin) receive stimuli from the environment and change them into nerve signals that are sent to the brain. It is the raw, basic detection of energy: light, sound waves, pressure, chemicals. Perception is the process by which the brain organises and interprets these sensory signals to give them meaning, so that we recognise objects, people and events.

Example: the eyes detect patches of colour and shape (sensation); the brain recognises them as a friend's face (perception). Hearing sounds is sensation; understanding them as the words of a song is perception.

Related points

  • Absolute threshold: the minimum stimulus that can be detected (a candle flame from some distance on a clear night).
  • Gestalt principles of perception: figure and ground, proximity, similarity, closure and continuity show how the brain organises a whole out of parts.
  • Perceptual set and illusions: the same stimulus can be perceived differently; in the Müller-Lyer illusion two equal lines look unequal, which shows that perception is not a copy of reality.

Sensation and perception are two parts of one continuous process: without sensation there is nothing to perceive, and without perception sensations have no meaning.

14. Explain attention and concentration with suitable examples

Attention is the process of selecting some information from the many stimuli around us and focusing mental effort on it, while ignoring the rest. It acts like a spotlight or filter. In a noisy canteen you still hear your friend speaking (the cocktail party effect) because you attend to her voice. Concentration is the ability to keep attention fixed on one object or task for a continuous period of time, without being distracted. Attention is the starting point; concentration is attention that is held steady.

Types of attention

  • Selective: focusing on one thing and ignoring others (reading in a noisy room).
  • Divided: attending to two things at the same time, which lowers performance (texting while driving).
  • Sustained: maintaining focus for a long period (a 3-hour exam, a lecture).
  • Alternating: shifting focus between tasks (switching between a lecture and note-making).

Factors affecting attention

External factors (stimulus)Internal factors (person)
Intensity and size (a loud noise, a bright sign)Interest and motives (a cricket fan notices the score)
Novelty and contrastNeeds and emotions (hungry people notice food)
Movement and repetitionMental set, expectation and habit

Improving concentration

  • Remove distractions (phone silent, tidy desk) and study at a fixed time and place.
  • Work in short blocks with breaks (for example 25 minutes of work and 5 minutes of rest, the Pomodoro method).
  • Set a clear goal for each session, and choose tasks that are interesting or make them meaningful.
  • Sleep well, exercise, and practise meditation, which trains sustained attention.

Importance: attention decides what is learned and remembered, and poor concentration is a major cause of errors and low marks; it is vital in driving, surgery, programming and every exam.

15. What is thinking? Explain the concept of thinking with suitable examples.

Thinking is the mental process of manipulating information (ideas, images, concepts, symbols and language) in order to understand, solve problems, make decisions, plan and imagine. It goes on inside the mind and cannot be seen directly, but we infer it from what people say and do. It is the core of cognition and the activity that separates humans from many other living beings.

Building blocks of thought

  • Concepts: mental categories, such as “bird” or “algorithm”, that let us group things and think economically.
  • Images: mental pictures (visualising a route home).
  • Language and symbols: words and numbers that express and organise thoughts.

Importance

Thinking underlies problem solving, planning, learning, creativity and decision making. Its quality improves with knowledge, practice, open-mindedness and awareness of biases, and it is reduced by stress, fatigue, prejudice and rigid habits.

16. Define memory. Explain the different types of memory with suitable examples.

Memory is the mental process of encoding (taking in), storing and retrieving information over time. It lets us learn from experience, recognise people and carry out skills. Without it every moment would feel brand new.

Atkinson–Shiffrin (multi-store) model

TypeDuration and capacityExample
Sensory memoryLess than 1–3 seconds; very large but fades quickly. Only what is attended to moves onThe afterimage of a flash, the echo of a word just spoken
Short-term (working) memoryAbout 15–30 seconds, about 7 ± 2 items; rehearsal keeps it longerHolding a phone number while dialling it
Long-term memoryAlmost unlimited in capacity and can last a lifetimeYour name, birthday, how to ride a cycle

Types of long-term memory

  • Explicit (declarative): consciously recalled.
    • Episodic: personal events (your first day in college).
    • Semantic: facts and general knowledge (the capital of India is Delhi).
  • Implicit (non-declarative): works without conscious effort.
    • Procedural: skills and habits (typing, playing the guitar).
    • Priming and conditioned responses: earlier exposure influencing later response.

Processes: encoding (converting information to a storable form), storage (maintaining it) and retrieval (recall, recognition, relearning). Rehearsal and meaningful organisation transfer information from short-term to long-term memory.

17. Write about critical thinking? Explain its importance with suitable examples

Critical thinking is the disciplined process of actively analysing, questioning and evaluating information, arguments and assumptions before accepting them or acting on them. A critical thinker does not simply believe what is read or heard; he or she asks: What is the evidence? Who says so, and why? Is the reasoning sound? What else could explain it?

Key skills

  • Analysis: breaking a problem or argument into parts.
  • Evaluation: judging the credibility of sources and the strength of evidence.
  • Inference: drawing logical conclusions.
  • Open-mindedness: considering other viewpoints and recognising one's own biases (confirmation bias, stereotyping).

Importance with examples

AreaHow critical thinking helps
StudiesUnderstanding concepts instead of memorising; judging which answer or source is reliable
EngineeringTesting assumptions in a design, finding the real cause of a bug instead of patching symptoms
Daily lifeChecking a forwarded message or advertisement for truth before sharing it, avoiding scams and fake news
Decision makingComparing options on facts, not emotions or peer pressure
CareerEmployers value people who solve problems independently and question weak plans
SocietyResists propaganda and prejudice, supports fair judgement

Example: an advertisement claims “9 out of 10 doctors recommend this tonic”. A critical thinker asks how many doctors were asked, who paid for the survey, and whether there is independent evidence.

Developing it: ask questions, read from varied sources, discuss and debate, separate facts from opinions, and reflect on one's own thinking.

18. What is forgetting? Explain the concept of forgetting with suitable examples.

Forgetting is the inability to recall or recognise information that was earlier learned and stored. It is a normal process, not always a defect: the mind drops unimportant details so that useful information can be used efficiently.

Ebbinghaus forgetting curve

Hermann Ebbinghaus, by memorising nonsense syllables, showed that forgetting is fastest soon after learning and then slows down. He found roughly 50–60% of new material is lost within the first day if it is not revised, while the rest fades slowly afterwards. Revision at spaced intervals flattens the curve.

Reducing forgetting

Revise at spaced intervals, understand rather than memorise, use mnemonics, organise material, sleep well, and recreate the learning context during recall.

19. Define creativity. Explain creativity with suitable examples.

Creativity is the ability to produce ideas, solutions or products that are both new (original) and useful or valuable. It is not limited to art; an engineer who finds a cheaper way to build a circuit or a student who finds a shorter proof is also creative.

Characteristics (Guilford)

  • Fluency: number of ideas produced (ten uses for a brick).
  • Flexibility: variety of categories of ideas (building, weapon, paperweight, pigment).
  • Originality: uncommon, unique ideas.
  • Elaboration: developing an idea in detail.

Stages of the creative process (Wallas)

Example: Archimedes studied the problem of the king's crown (preparation), set it aside (incubation), realised the displacement of water in his bath (illumination) and then tested it (verification).

Examples

  • Art and music: a new song composed from old folk tunes.
  • Science and engineering: the smartphone combining phone, camera and computer; solar-powered street lamps.
  • Everyday: turning waste bottles into plant pots or finding a new study method.

Barriers and ways to improve

Fear of failure, strict rules, criticism and lack of curiosity block creativity. It improves through brainstorming, asking “what if”, mind maps, trying new hobbies, taking breaks, and a safe environment in which mistakes are allowed.

20. What is decision making? Explain the decision-making process with suitable examples.

Decision making is the mental process of choosing one course of action from two or more alternatives in order to reach a goal. It involves thinking, judging and accepting responsibility for the result. Decisions range from small (what to eat) to life-changing (which branch or job to choose).

Steps in the process

  1. Identify the decision: know exactly what must be decided and why.
  2. Collect information: facts, advice and personal values.
  3. List the alternatives: as many realistic options as possible.
  4. Weigh the alternatives: advantages, disadvantages, risks, cost and time.
  5. Choose the best alternative.
  6. Take action: put the choice into practice with a plan.
  7. Review: check the result and learn; change course if needed.

Example: a final-year student must choose between a job offer and a master's course. She lists the options, collects data on salary, fees and growth, compares pros and cons against her long-term goal, picks the job and later reviews whether to study part-time.

Factors and pitfalls

  • Influencing factors: values, emotions, past experience, family and peer opinion, time pressure and available information.
  • Biases: confirmation bias (looking only for supporting facts), anchoring (being stuck to the first figure seen), overconfidence and herd behaviour.
  • Styles: rational (logical analysis), intuitive (gut feeling), dependent (relies on others) and avoidant (postpones).

Good decisions come from clear goals, adequate information, and a calm mind; a decision tree or a weighted decision matrix can support the choice.
